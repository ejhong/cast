ABOUT THIS MODELA conceptual specimen with an exaggerated seam for visibility. The amount of leakage is prescribed, not calculated from a tested mixture. The timber texture is procedural; it is not copied from an ancient surface.

THE EXPLANATORY IDEA

What could this
make easier?

Watch material enter a narrow joint in a wooden form. When the boards part, a small fin remains on the stone, along with the suggestion of wood grain.

01

A raised line across a face

A narrow opening between mold boards gives material somewhere to protrude, creating a fin when it hardens.

Still to explainA surviving ridge can also come from unfinished dressing, a mineral vein, repair material, or differential weathering.

02

Board-like surface detail

A form can transfer shallow texture during contact, so a single operation could produce both the face and its fine detail.

Still to explainThe grain direction and relief need close documentation. A younger render or repair could carry a mold impression too.

03

Similar marks on several blocks

Reusing the same form could repeat a distinctive joint or defect on more than one cast surface.

Still to explainA useful match needs dimensions and the same irregular details, rather than several roughly straight lines.

FROM POSSIBILITY TO EVIDENCE

How would
we tell?

The proposal

This scene isolates one proposed consequence of timber formwork. A seam and its adjacent texture might record contact with a mold, but this invented specimen makes no claim about the origin of a particular ancient block.

The comparison

Compare formwork traces with stone-dressing marks, natural bands, weathering, and repair seams. Establish that the feature belongs to the original stone surface before interpreting it as evidence of manufacture.

A USEFUL PHYSICAL TEST

Cast matched specimens against tight and deliberately open board joints. Record mix consistency, gap size, curing, and release. Scan the resulting fins and grain, then compare a documented candidate using the same surface measurements.
